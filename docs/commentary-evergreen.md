#### Segment 1
**Suggested duration:** ~45 s (≈ 125 words) · intro phase, before first move

Alright, WELCOME to Phantasm Chess! We are going to Berlin — 1852. Not a tournament. Not a championship match. Just a casual game between two people who know each other, played freely, with no prize and no clock pressure.

But what Adolf Anderssen creates here is so clean, so elegant, and so brutally exact that Wilhelm Steinitz — the first official World Chess Champion — will look back decades later and give it a name: the Evergreen Game. A game that never withers. One year after the Immortal Game, Anderssen is still at the peak of Romantic chess, still turning ordinary evenings into something history refuses to forget.

#### Segment 2
**Suggested duration:** ~55 s (≈ 155 words) · plies 1–15, player context over opening

Anderssen is thirty-four years old. A mathematics professor from Breslau. And he plays chess the way a mathematician attacks a proof: not by hoping for beauty, but by calculating until the beauty becomes unavoidable.

His style is Romantic chess: open lines, active pieces, sacrifice first if the attack is real. Across from him is Jean Dufresne, a serious German chess writer and student of the game. He is not a beginner, not a victim wandering into danger by accident.

That matters, because the Evergreen Game is not famous because Black collapses instantly. It is famous because Dufresne keeps finding moves that look natural. He takes material. He develops enough to believe he can survive. He gives Anderssen exactly the kind of target a Romantic attacker wants: a king that looks safe until the lines around it suddenly open.

The opening is an Evans Gambit. Anderssen gives up a pawn to pull Black forward and open the position. Dufresne accepts, takes material, and for a while everything looks playable. That is exactly the point. Anderssen is not rushing. He is arranging the board.

#### Segment 3
**Suggested duration:** ~43 s (≈ 120 words) · plies 16–30, tension buildup

This is the difference between the Evergreen and the Immortal. The Immortal explodes immediately. This game tightens.

Dufresne wins pawns. Anderssen wins time. Dufresne's material count looks good. Anderssen's pieces keep improving. The king stays in the center, the files begin to matter, and every white piece starts leaning toward the same target.

By move sixteen, Black is still ahead in material and still seems to be holding. But the position has changed character. The extra pawns no longer feel like wealth. They feel like weight. And then Anderssen plays knight to f6.

#### Segment 4
**Suggested duration:** ~47 s (≈ 135 words) · plies 31–45, compressed tactical sequence

Move seventeen. Knight to f6. Check. Dufresne takes it, and the kingside cracks open.

Soon after, Anderssen throws in the rook. Rook to e7. Black can take it, and Black does. Then the queen steps to d7, right in front of the king. Black can take that too, and Black does.

Knight gone. Rook gone. Queen gone. To the eye, it looks impossible. To Anderssen, it is already finished. Bishop to f5. Check. Bishop to d7. Check. Bishop takes e7. Checkmate. The king is trapped not by force alone, but by geometry: two bishops closing the diagonals, every escape square already accounted for.

#### Segment 5
**Suggested duration:** ~23 s (≈ 65 words) · final recap + epilogue

That is why this game lasts. Not because Anderssen sacrifices material, but because every sacrifice has a job. The knight opens lines. The rook drags pieces away. The queen pulls the king onto the fatal square.

The Evergreen Game is Romantic chess at its cleanest: not chaos, not luck, not spectacle for spectacle's sake. Calculation made visible. A final position that looks less like a trick, and more like something that had been waiting there from move one.
