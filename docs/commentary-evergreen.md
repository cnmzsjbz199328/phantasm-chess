Checkmate on move twenty-four. With two bishops and geometry. Three sacrifices that looked like recklessness and turned out to be absolute necessity — every piece, every square, every escape route calculated in advance.

Steinitz saw this game thirty years later and felt that it had aged without a single wrinkle. Anderssen's Romantic games were always spectacular — the sacrifices were always theatrical — but this one had something underneath the spectacle that most of them didn't: an economy of motion, a structural logic so clean that the final position looks inevitable rather than miraculous. He called it the Evergreen Game and wrote that it would endure as long as chess itself endured.

He was not wrong. This game is still shown to students. Still reproduced in textbooks. Still recognizable by anyone who has spent serious time with the game. Jean Dufresne's king has been sitting on f8 for a hundred and seventy years, staring at those two bishops, trying to find the way out. There is no way out.
