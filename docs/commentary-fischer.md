The chess culture of 1956 is completely unprepared for him. The Soviets own the world championship and have for a decade. Many people believe the Soviet training system produces something unbeatable. #### Segment 4
**Suggested duration:** ~95 s (≈ 265 words) · the queen sacrifice and forced sequence; keep the cadence deliberate

Move seventeen. Fischer slides his dark-squared bishop to e6. Quietly. No fireworks. No immediate check. Just a bishop, moving to e6.

But what it means is this: Byrne's queen has been sitting deep in Black's position capturing material — and now Fischer's queen on b6 is left completely undefended, hanging right in front of Byrne's bishop on c5. Byrne can just take it. He does. He snaps up Fischer's queen. He is now UP a queen. Against a thirteen-year-old kid who just gave away his most valuable piece.

And then Fischer plays bishop to c4. Check. The king moves. Knight to e2. Check. King moves back. Knight to d4. Check. King scurries again. Knight checks again.

This is the part that makes the game legendary. Fischer is not gambling that the attack will work. He is reading a forced sequence while the room is still reacting to the missing queen. Every time Byrne's king moves, another black piece arrives with tempo. Every defense collapses into the next check. The material balance stops mattering because Byrne is not choosing anymore. He is being carried.

What Fischer saw on move seventeen was a long combination: every check, every king escape, every defensive attempt, and the material count waiting on the other side. By move twenty-five, Byrne still technically has his queen, but Fischer has two rooks, a bishop, and absolute control.
